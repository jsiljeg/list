# Pairing principles

What a working sommelier weighs, roughly in order of how often it decides the
answer. The house model covers the first three through tags and styles; the
rest is where your judgement earns its place.

## 1. Weight meets weight
Delicate food, delicate wine; rich food, rich wine. Body, alcohol and texture
should be in the same register as the dish. A crudo needs a Grk or a Chablis,
not a Barolo; a braised beef cheek flattens a Graševina. **This is the first
filter and the most common error.**

## 2. Pair the sauce and the method, not the protein
"Chicken" says almost nothing. Poached with beurre blanc → rich or mineral
white. Roast with jus → Pinot Noir. Cacciatore with tomato → Sangiovese.
Cooking method moves weight up: raw < steamed < poached < sautéed < roasted <
grilled < braised < smoked. Charred and smoked edges invite oak and riper fruit.

## 3. Acid
- Wine acidity must at least match the food's (tomato, citrus, vinaigrette).
  A low-acid wine beside a sharp dish tastes flat and flabby.
- Acid **cuts fat and fried crumb** — schnitzel, fritto misto, burrata,
  pork belly, mantecato risotto. Sparkling does it twice: acid and bubbles.
- Acid **loves salt**: salty food makes a high-acid wine taste rounder.

## 4. Tannin
- Tannin needs **protein and fat** to grip — steak softens a young Cabernet.
- Tannin **plus iodine/fish oil reads metallic** (oysters, caviar, mackerel).
- Tannin is **amplified by salt-free bitterness and by heat** (chili), and
  made harsher by sweetness in the dish.
- Salt softens tannin — why Dingač and pršut work.

## 5. Sugar
- The wine must be **at least as sweet as the dish**, or it tastes thin and
  sour. Fruit desserts are the softest test; chocolate the hardest.
- A touch of sugar **tames chili heat** and partners salt (blue cheese and
  Sauternes; spicy Thai and off-dry Riesling).

## 6. Heat, spice and alcohol
Alcohol inflames capsaicin. For spicy food: lower alcohol, a little residual
sugar, aromatic grapes (Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Muškat), no hard tannin, no
heavy oak.

## 7. Umami and bitterness — the hard ones
- **Umami** (aged Parmesan, mushrooms, soy, cured meat, tomato) makes wine
  taste more bitter and tannic and less fruity. Answer: soft tannin, fruit,
  acid, a pinch of salt in the dish. Champagne and aged whites handle it well.
- **Bitterness in food** (radicchio, char, dark greens) stacks on tannin.
- **Artichoke and asparagus** make wine taste sweet/metallic: bone-dry,
  high-acid, unoaked (Grüner, Sauvignon, dry Riesling, brut sparkling).
- **Eggs** coat the palate: sparkling, or a mineral white.
- **Vinegar** fights everything; sparkling or a sharp white survives.

## 8. Oak
Oak wants richness, cream, butter, smoke and roast. It clashes with raw,
steamed, citrus and very delicate fish, and with spice.

## 9. Bridge ingredients
Pull the wine toward the dish through one shared note: mushrooms → Pinot Noir
and aged Nebbiolo; truffle → Nebbiolo, white Burgundy, Malvazija istarska;
herbs → Sauvignon; black pepper → Syrah; citrus → Riesling; brown butter →
oaked Chardonnay; cherry → Pinot.

## 10. Contrast or complement — choose on purpose
Complement: buttery dish, buttery wine. Contrast: fatty dish, sharp wine;
salty dish, sweet wine. Both work; mixing them by accident does not.

## 11. What grows together goes together
Regional pairing is a strong prior with centuries of testing behind it: Plavac
mali and peka lamb; Malvazija istarska and Istrian truffle and fish; Pošip and
Grk with Dalmatian shellfish; Teran and pršut; Graševina and freshwater fish;
Chablis and oysters; Barolo and tajarin al tartufo; Sancerre and chèvre.

## 12. Temperature and order
Serve whites cold enough to be fresh but not numb (8–12 °C), reds cellar-cool
(14–18 °C); a red that is too warm tastes alcoholic beside food. Across a menu:
light before heavy, dry before sweet, young before old, white before red, unless
the dish says otherwise.

## Classic safe harbours
- **Champagne / brut sparkling**: fried food, salty starters, eggs, sushi,
  oysters, caviar, tartare, jamón. When in doubt, Champagne rarely fails.
- **Pinot Noir**: duck, mushrooms, salmon/tuna, poultry, veal.
- **Riesling (dry to off-dry)**: pork, Asian, spice, smoked fish.
- **Chardonnay (oaked)**: lobster, poultry in cream, risotto, roast fish.
- **Sangiovese**: tomato, pasta, pizza, cured meat.
- **Sweet wine**: blue cheese, foie gras, fruit tarts, nuts.

## Things that sound right and are wrong
- "Red with meat, white with fish": tuna steak with Pinot Noir is right;
  creamy veal with Chardonnay is right.
- "Big wine for a special dish": the most expensive bottle is rarely the best
  pairing; match the dish, then trade up within the right style.
- "Dessert wine with any dessert": a light fruit dessert can be flattened by
  Sauternes; Moscato d'Asti or a Brut rosé sparkling may be the answer.
